These sheets distinguish the version or testimony that conveys a motif, the sources of context and the literary recreation of this remedy. A documented legend doesn't prove his events happened. The date of the editions does not amount to the date of creation of the testimony.

Tenerife

Traditional or documented element: A modern account of the legend associated with Mount Teide tells how Achamán freed Magec from Guayota.

Source:

  • Government of the Canary Islands (n.d.). Historia del Parque Nacional del Teide. See “La leyenda de Guayota y el Pan de Azúcar”.

This page records a modern version. The date of individual episodes remains uncertain. Distinguish the earliest accounts of Echeyde and Guayota from later retellings.

About this version: This story’s Dácil is an original character distinct from the Dácil in Antonio de Viana’s poem. Her work with the herd, her brother, the dialogue and the refrain were created for MitoMapa.

Gran Canaria

Traditional or documented element: Accounts describe tibicenas in a variety of animal forms. Written accounts from after the conquest describe one form as a large dog.

Source:

  • Corrales Zumbado, C., and Corbella Díaz, D. (2013). Diccionario histórico del español de Canarias: tibicena. The entry “tibicena” cites Gómez Escudero, 1978 edition, p. 439, and Marín de Cubas, 1993 edition, book II, ch. 18, p. 204.

The chroniclers’ religious language also reflects their Christian perspective. These sources record beliefs about the creature. The story uses one of the forms described in those accounts.

About this version: The story’s Guayarmina is fictional and shares her name with a historical figure. The herd, the creature’s three circuits, the footprints, Guayarmina’s appeal to Acorán and the goatherds’ response were written for the story.

La Palma

Traditional or documented element: A chronicle records fears that Idafe might fall and describes offerings left by the rock. The park’s account places these traditions in the Caldera.

Source:

  • Trapero, M., and Santana Martel, E. (n.d.). Diccionario de toponimia de Canarias: Idafe. The entry “Idafe” cites Abreu Galindo, 1977 edition, p. 270.
  • Government of the Canary Islands (n.d.). Caldera de Taburiente: Arqueología. See the sections on the Benahoarita people and rituals at Idafe.

The cited passage leaves the details of the ceremony and its possible connection to Abora unverified. Treat the ceremony in the story as fiction.

About this version: This Acerina differs from the character in other versions. Her grandfather, the pots, the tremor, the promise and the refrain are fictional. “Holding up the sky” is an image created for the story.

La Gomera

Traditional or documented element: Virgilio Brito’s version, recorded by Trapero, includes Jonay’s journey using skins to stay afloat, the couple’s love and their tragic end. Gara’s reflection and the contrast between water and fire also appear in Los Chorros de Epina, published by Spain’s Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food.

Sources:

  • Trapero, M. (1984). La leyenda de Gara y Jonay en La Gomera. Aguayro, 153, pp. 4–6. Virgilio Brito’s version appears on pp. 5–6.
  • Navarro Mederos, J. F. (2016). Arqueología en La Gomera: lo que va de ayer a hoy. pp. 13–38.
  • Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food (n.d.). Los Chorros de Epina. Caminos Naturales. See the section on the legend of Gara and Jonay. Identified during the supplementary review on 1 November 2026. Consultation during the writing of the story is unconfirmed.

The cited version by Brito omits the water and fire motif. Other publications include it, but the motif’s origin, age and direct source in this story remain unconfirmed.

About this version: This version simplifies the events and leaves the tragic ending implicit. The refrain, the reflection on dialogue and the appeal to Orahan were written for this retelling.

El Hierro

Traditional or documented element: The Garoé is associated with collecting water from mist. A modern account of the legend includes Agarfa and the concealment of the tree.

Source:

  • Government of the Canary Islands (n.d.). Leyenda del garoé. CanariWiki. See “Historia”.
  • El Hierro Travel (n.d.). Árbol Garoé. See “El árbol sagrado de El Hierro”.

Separate the natural process of collecting water from mist from the events told in the legend. The age of those details remains uncertain.

About this version: Agarfa is portrayed here as a child who protects the path, a different character from the Agarfa in the linked account. Her route, the conversations and the refrain were created for this story.

Fuerteventura

Traditional or documented element: Oral accounts of Mafasca describe the burning of a cross and the appearance of the light. Versions and explanations vary.

Source:

  • Carreras Navarro, J. (2016). La Luz de Mafasca. pp. 285–310. Accounts involving a cross appear on pp. 293 and 297–298.

The cross offers insufficient evidence to date the whole tradition. The accounts collected near Tindaya leave its possible Indigenous origins unverified. The cause of the light remains open in the story.

About this version: Guise and Ayose are fictional goatherds who share their names with historical leaders. The gofio, the dialogue, the promise to repair the cross and the refrain were chosen for this retelling.

Lanzarote

Traditional or documented element: Abreu Galindo’s account includes the trial by smoke, the help of an older woman and the sponge. Pérez Saavedra reproduces and studies this account.

Sources:

  • Pérez Saavedra, F. (1995). La aventura de Avendaño y la leyenda de Ico. Anuario de Estudios Atlánticos, 41, pp. 207–226. The trial and the older woman’s help are discussed on pp. 223–224.

The study discusses Ico’s parentage and records the claim that Avendaño was her biological father. The story simplifies her parentage by calling her the daughter of Zonzamas and Fayna. Her genealogy remains unverified.

About this version: This retelling simplifies the conflict and places Ico at the centre. Her words, her question to the council and the refrain were written for this version.

La Graciosa

Traditional or documented element: San Borondón appears on historical maps and in travellers’ accounts. Chaparro records some claims of landings, all of which remain unverified. The tradition is linked to the legendary voyage of Saint Brendan.

Sources:

  • Chaparro Domínguez, M. Á. (2013). Revisión del mito geográfico de San Borondón y aproximación a su huella en la literatura y otras artes. Revista de Filología Románica, 30(2), pp. 229–244. Origins and travellers’ accounts are discussed on pp. 232–236.
  • Martínez Berriel, S. (2001–2002). La isla de las buenas costumbres: cambio social y cultural en la isla de La Graciosa. Vegueta, 6. See the abstract and the discussion of settlement.
  • William Turpin, Dickinson College Commentaries. Introduction — The Voyage of Saint Brendan the Abbot. Consulted for the editorial review on 1 November 2026.
  • Dickinson College Commentaries, edited by William Turpin. Latin Text of Navigatio Brendani. Consulted for the editorial review on 1 November 2026.
  • Luis Regueira Benítez (El Museo Canario) and Manuel Poggio Capote (Real Sociedad Cosmológica). El capellán Juan Díaz, «obispo de San Borondón». Nuevas noticias sobre la exploración del Atlántico (1526–1529). Consulted for the editorial review on 1 November 2026.
  • Direção Cultural da Marinha, Museu de Marinha. Caravela latina — modelo MM.05724. Consulted for the editorial review on 1 November 2026.
  • Armada Española. Los tipos de naos en la época de los descubrimientos y exploraciones, siglos XV–XVII. Consulted for the editorial review on 1 November 2026.

The story’s line “no one managed to reach it” simplifies the reports of searches and alleged landings. None of the landings has been verified. The episode near La Graciosa is fictional. Accounts usually place San Borondón west of the Canary Islands.

About this version: This version retains the original story and narration. Guadarfía is a fictional apprentice sailor distinct from the historical leader of Lanzarote. The illustrations show a small two-masted caravel with lateen sails, a plausible vessel for the early 16th century. The captain, the drawing, the dialogue and the refrain were created for this story.

Heritage sources for the passport pieces

  • Government of the Canary Islands (n.d.). Canarian Amazigh ceramics by island. Infographic referred to as visual reference for the seven receptacles of the passport.

The illustrations are contemporary recreations, not exact reproductions of archaeological pieces. The choice of an island form does not mean that it was its only ceramic form. El Hierro has also been using a photo of vessels supplied by the authority, without identifying a concrete excavated piece.

La Graciosa snola is a contemporary symbol of its link with the sea and does not appear as an archaeological tipology documented on the island or as a form attached to it by infography. The external documents retain their original conditions.

Island names: sources and uncertainties

Selection for the game, added on 20 / 10 / 2026. According to information from the complainant, this relationship has been contrasted with expert people at the pre-Hispanic age of Canary Islands. It's a contribution communicated by the complainant, different from his bibliographic references to his stories. It's not presented as an academic opinion or as a consensus about all his denominations.

The names referred are not completely secure: the photographs vary from testimony, often written after the conquest and do not always allow their original form, meaning, seniority or scope to be established. It should not be assumed that all these forms are documented at the same time or that each island had a single name. The game uses the following didactic selection, open to review:
